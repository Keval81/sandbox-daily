# Buying the Ceiling

Liverpool have reportedly put something close to €100m on the table for a footballer who has not yet finished being a teenager. The fee — roughly £87m — is not for a proven match-winner. It is for **Yan Diomande**, born on 14 November 2006, which means he is nineteen years old and has been alive for fewer years than some Premier League full-backs have been professionals.

This is not a story about a teenager being "linked" with a big club. Teenagers get linked with big clubs every week, the way weather happens. The story is the number. And the number tells you something genuinely strange about how the modern game decides what a human being is worth.

## What we actually know

Let's keep this simple, because the facts are simple.

Diomande is an Ivorian winger, 1.82m tall, currently at RB Leipzig. He arrived there in 2025. His route to the top is the kind of thing that sounds invented: born in Ivory Coast, he moved to the United States as a teenager, came up through DME Academy, then had a spell and a breakthrough at Leganés before Leipzig signed him. He now has senior involvement with the Ivory Coast national team.

Liverpool are heavily linked. They are reported to have already had an opening bid rejected. Leipzig, predictably, want to keep him — possibly by improving his contract with some sort of future release-clause arrangement, which is football-speak for "we'll let you leave later, for even more, once we've squeezed another season of value out of you." Diomande himself is said to be tempted to go now. And lurking in the background, because they are always lurking in the background, are Paris Saint-Germain.

That's the picture. Now here's why it matters.

## You are not buying a player. You're buying a forecast.

Here is the uncomfortable truth that a €100m bid for a 19-year-old exposes: at the very top of the market, clubs have largely stopped paying for what a player has done. They are paying for what they have decided he might become.

This is a profound shift, and most coverage glides right past it. When you spend nine figures on a teenager with barely a year of Bundesliga football behind him, you are not buying goals or assists or any measurable thing. You are buying a story about the future — a bet that the ceiling is high enough to justify the cost. The output is almost beside the point. The **potential** is the product.

And that should make you a little uneasy, because potential is the one thing in football that cannot be verified until it's too late to get your money back.

## The house with a nice view

Think of it like buying property, because that's basically what this is.

There are two ways to spend a fortune on a winger right now, and Liverpool appear to be shopping for both at the same time.

Option one is the finished house. That's Bradley Barcola — the other winger Liverpool's interest has surfaced around at exactly the same moment. Barcola is described as the more established, more "ready-made" option. You walk in, the kitchen works, the roof doesn't leak, and you know precisely what you're getting. The catch is that everyone else can see the kitchen works too, so the finished house costs a fortune, and you're bidding against every other buyer who also wants to move in tomorrow.

Option two is the empty plot of land with a spectacular view. That's Diomande. There's no house yet. There might never be a house. But the *view* — the raw athletic gift, the ceiling — is the kind that makes property developers lose their composure. You're not paying for what's there. You're paying for what you're convinced could be built there. And if you're right, you've got the most valuable thing on the street. If you're wrong, you've paid house money for a field.

Spending €100m on the plot of land rather than the house is, when you say it out loud, faintly insane. It is also completely normal now. That's the tell about where the whole market's head is at.

## The domino sitting on Diomande's desk

Now for the part that turns this from a single transfer into a system.

Notice that PSG appear in *both* of Liverpool's winger stories. They're interested in Diomande. And Barcola's entire situation is described as depending heavily on PSG's own planning, because Barcola is theirs. One French club is sitting at the centre of two separate deals like a spider feeling vibrations on two different threads.

This is how the elite winger market actually works, and it has very little to do with talent. It runs on **leverage**.

Picture the dominoes. PSG decide what to do with Barcola. That decision determines whether Barcola is even available, and at what price. Whether Barcola is available shapes whether Liverpool push hard for the ready-made option or pivot to the upside gamble. Whether Liverpool pivot to Diomande shapes how confidently Leipzig can hold out for that improved-contract, future-clause stalling tactic. And whether Leipzig hold firm shapes whether PSG — who also fancy Diomande — get a clear run or a bidding war.

Round and round it goes. Each club's move is the input to every other club's decision. Nobody is acting on the merits of the footballer in front of them. They're acting on what they think the *other* buyers and sellers will do. The player, the actual nineteen-year-old human being who just wants to play, is the last variable anyone's looking at.

## Liverpool's usual supermarket

There's one more thread worth pulling, and it's the least surprising fact in the entire saga: Liverpool have repeatedly bought or tracked players from Red Bull environments. Diomande plays for RB Leipzig. A Red Bull club.

So a Premier League side that treats the Red Bull pipeline like its preferred supermarket finds itself "heavily linked" with a Red Bull winger. At this point that's less a transfer rumour than a weather forecast. If anything, the genuinely shocking version of this story would be Liverpool *not* being linked with a talented attacker at a Red Bull club. That would be the news.

## The point

Strip away the noise and here's what you're left with. A €100m bid for a teenager is not a quirk or a wild overpay — it is the market telling you, plainly, what it now values. Not what a player has done. Not even, really, who he is. It values the size of the forecast and the strength of your negotiating position relative to everyone else circling the same names.

Diomande may turn out to be magnificent. He may justify every penny and make this look like a bargain. But whether he ends up at Liverpool, at PSG, or stuck in Leipzig on a shiny new contract won't be decided by how good he is. It'll be decided by what PSG do with Barcola, by how long Leipzig can hold their nerve, and by who blinks first in a game of leverage being played several rooms away from him.

That's the modern transfer market in one sentence: the player is the prize, and almost the only person in the building with no say in where he goes.
