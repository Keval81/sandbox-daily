# Jeff Bezos Finally Found a Sports Team to Buy. It Was Never Going to Be American.

Jeff Bezos can afford almost anything on Earth, and for years he failed to buy one specific thing: a sports team. He reportedly looked at a U.S. football team once and never closed the deal. The lesson turned out not to be that he lacked the money. It was that he was shopping on the wrong continent.

## The £1.35 Billion Rumor

On August 10, 2026, TechCrunch — citing *The Guardian* — reported that Bezos is part of a group trying to buy at least a 30% stake in Liverpool FC. The price on the stake is £1.35 billion, roughly $1.8 billion. Gross that up and the whole club works out to around £4.5 billion, or about $6 billion — which happens to sit right on top of Forbes' valuation of Liverpool. The group reportedly includes Facebook co-founder Eduardo Saverin, plus others the report doesn't name.

A necessary warning before anyone gets carried away: this is reported, not confirmed. No club statement. No named seller. No timeline. Treat it as a loud rumor with a precise number bolted onto it.

But the number is the interesting part, and so is the destination. Because the real story here isn't a billionaire buying a toy, and it certainly isn't Americans discovering football. It's that Liverpool has become something rarer — the last liquid trophy asset in sport, and the reason the richest Americans keep landing in England to grab one.

## The Members-Only Club That Stopped Taking Applications

Americans didn't fall in love with the beautiful game. They ran the numbers and noticed that top-tier English clubs are the only elite sports assets still actually for sale.

Look at what they can't buy at home. NFL and MLB franchises are either too expensive or simply closed off to new buyers. U.S. leagues run on restrictive ownership rules and a finite, cartel-controlled supply — existing owners vote on who gets to join, and mostly they vote no. You cannot walk up and buy a slice of the Yankees, no matter how many hundreds of billions you have. It's a members-only club that quietly stopped taking applications.

The Premier League is the opposite kind of business. It lets you buy a stake and hold a minority position. The lights are on and the shop is open.

And here is the single statistic that tells the whole story: 13 of the 20 Premier League clubs already have American shareholders, according to Sky Sports. That is not a trend anymore. That is a takeover with a scoreboard.

## Two Markets, One Obvious Trade

And crucially, the U.S. audience for soccer keeps growing, which means the value of the thing you're buying is still rising while you buy it. If you own distribution companies, you know exactly what you're looking at.

## Why Distribution People Want a Football Club

The men circling English clubs aren't just fans with deep pockets — they're people who built empires out of moving content and attention around. Bezos built Amazon, which has already streamed European soccer. Saverin helped build Facebook. Todd Boehly, who owns Chelsea, also owns the LA Dodgers and the LA Lakers. These are distribution people, and a football club isn't merely a team to them. It's a content factory that happens to play matches.

The proof of concept is already famous. Ryan Reynolds' *Welcome to Wrexham* is a documentary about owning an English club — and it, along with *Ted Lasso*, made the whole idea romantic. Reynolds bought a fourth-tier club and turned the act of owning it into the product. The football was almost a subplot.

Now take that thesis and crank it to maximum. Wrexham was a lovable underdog in the fourth division — a charming indie film. Liverpool carries Dallas Cowboys–level brand recognition in the U.K. That's not an indie film. That's a franchise blockbuster with a global audience already in its seats before the opening credits.

Ownership also buys access to some of the most bankable, recognizable icons on Earth — and increasingly those icons are buying in themselves. The current guest list of American owners reads like a fever dream. Tom Brady holds a piece of Birmingham City. Michael B. Jordan is in the group that owns Bournemouth. Will Ferrell, Russell Westbrook, and Michael Phelps jointly own Leeds United — a sentence that sounds less like a boardroom and more like the seating chart at a very confused wedding. Woody Johnson, co-owner of the NY Jets and a former U.S. Ambassador to the U.K., owns Crystal Palace. Boehly has Chelsea. Dan Friedkin holds a stake in Everton.

## The Bezos Tell

There's one more detail that turns this from a purchase into a plan. Since marrying Lauren Sánchez, Bezos is read less as a pure tech mogul and more as a cultural figure. His new AI company, Prometheus, is reportedly hunting a lease in King's Cross, London's AI hub. A football stake and an AI office in the same city, at the same time, is not a hobby. It's a man setting up a second life.

And for a man worth around $280 billion, the £1.35 billion price tag is essentially sofa-cushion money — a sum he could misplace and not feel. This was never about whether he could afford it. It was about finding a shelf with something left on it.

## The Template, Not the Toy

The tidy version of this story — a distant American billionaire falling for English football — is a pleasant fiction and mostly a decoy. What's really happening is colder and smarter. The people who own distribution have looked at English football clubs and concluded they are underpriced trophies with a media catalog attached, sold in an open market that their own leagues would never permit.

Bezos didn't lose his heart to Liverpool. He found the one aisle in global sport that still had inventory. If this deal closes, it won't be the strangest American purchase of an English club. It'll be the model everyone else copies next.
