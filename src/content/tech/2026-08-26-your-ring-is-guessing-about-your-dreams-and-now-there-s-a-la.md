# Your Ring Is Guessing About Your Dreams, and Now There's a Lawsuit About It

Somewhere, a lawyer has looked at the titanium band on your finger and decided it lied to you about your own sleep.

That's the short version of a class-action complaint recently filed against Oura, the company that makes the smart ring millions of people wear to bed. The charge: Oura duped its customers by claiming the ring can accurately tell you which stage of sleep you're in. The suit's argument is refreshingly blunt. A ring cannot accurately track sleep stages, it says, because it does not directly measure your brain waves. And the language sharpens from there. Oura, the complaint claims, uses AI models to exploit "desperate consumers who struggle with their sleep … by selling faulty AI-based inference as reliable science."

The verdict on the actual science? The complaint calls Oura's sleep staging **no more accurate than a "coin flip."**

## The Part Everyone's Getting Wrong

The easy laugh here is at the plaintiffs. Victoria Song, who has spent 13-plus years reviewing wearables and health tech after stints at Gizmodo and PC Magazine, took it herself. "When I first saw this lawsuit, I laughed it off," she wrote. Her objection was reasonable: the suit "assumes that Oura users aren't smart enough to know the ring on their finger can't measure what's going on inside their skull." Of course a ring can't read your brain. You knew that when you bought it. Everyone did.

Then, in Song's words, "I logged on and saw the discourse" — and the laugh died in her throat.

Here's the thing the finger-jokes miss. This case isn't really about whether people are dumb enough to think jewelry can telepathically audit their REM cycles. It's a test case for something much bigger and much more dangerous to the entire industry: the question of whether a company can dress up a statistical guess as **settled science** and sell it as a nightly medical-grade verdict on your body. If that's illegal — if "faulty AI-based inference sold as reliable science" is a thing a court will actually punish — then the ring is not the problem. The ring is the first domino.

Because every wearable does the same trick. Apple does it. Samsung, Garmin, Whoop, Fitbit — all of them. And the moment the logic sticks to one, it fits the rest like a glove.

## What Your Ring Actually Does All Night

Let's build this up from the bottom, because once you see the mechanism you can't unsee it.

Your ring does not see your sleep. It cannot. As Song puts it, "as a noninvasive tool, no wearable directly measures anything except motion." Noninvasive just means it sits on your skin and doesn't break it — no needles, no electrodes glued to your scalp. That's the whole appeal. It's also the whole limitation.

So what does it have to work with? Three things: motion, heart rate, and temperature. That's the entire toolkit. From those three signals it has to reconstruct the vastly more complicated thing happening in your head.

Think of it like a detective who is never allowed into the room. He stands in the hallway and watches the strip of light under the door. He can't see the crime. But he knows things. He knows that when people fall asleep, their heart rate slows. He knows body temperature rises and falls across the night in a predictable rhythm. He knows that during REM sleep — the dreaming stage — the body paralyzes its own muscles so you don't leap up and physically act out whatever nonsense your brain is producing. So the detective watches the shadows, notes that the heart has slowed and the body has gone still, and announces with total confidence: "REM. 1:42 a.m. Write it down."

He might be right. He's a good detective. But he is **inferring**, not observing. Inference is just a fancy word for an educated guess — you collect the clues you can get and reason your way to the answer you can't. Every health number your wearable shows you is one of these guesses: your heart-rate zones, your HRV, your stress score, your blood oxygen, your "readiness," your "recovery," your body-temperature deviation. Not one of them is measured directly. All of them are the detective in the hallway, reading shadows, feeding the clues into an algorithm, and handing you a number with a straight face.

That is not a scandal. That is how the technology works, and it can work well. Here's where Song makes the point the lawsuit conveniently skips over. These same noninvasive gadgets do something a hospital machine physically cannot: they watch you all day, every day, passively, without you strapping into a lab. "It's not always the most accurate," she writes, "but even broadly accurate data has improved — and even saved — many lives." A guess that's roughly right, made continuously and for free while you go about your life, can catch a problem no one-night lab test ever would.

## Where the Guess Becomes a Lie

So if inference is normal and even useful, where's the crime? It lives in one gap: the distance between "here's our best guess from three sensors" and "here is precisely what your brain did last night."

The honest version of this business, Song notes, is that responsible companies validate their metrics against clinical, gold-standard methods — the real, invasive, boring lab equipment that actually does measure the thing. You take your detective-in-the-hallway guess, you compare it against the truth thousands of times, and you find out how good the guess really is. If it holds up, you've earned the right to sell it as a screening tool.

The lawsuit's whole gambit is that Oura skipped that last step and sold the confidence anyway. "Faulty AI-based inference as reliable science" is a precise accusation. It doesn't say the ring is useless. It says the company took a coin-flip and printed it on the box as a fact.

And that is exactly why this one matters beyond Oura. The complaint didn't target a general shopper — it named "desperate consumers who struggle with their sleep." People who can't sleep don't want a fun estimate. They want an answer, they'll pay for an answer, and they are the least equipped to argue when a glowing app tells them, with a number to two decimal places, that their night was a disaster. That's the vulnerability. A confident number aimed at a frightened customer is a very different product from a fitness toy.

## The Domino

Song's framing question is the one that should keep every wearable executive awake — ironically, the one thing their own devices can't measure. "A new lawsuit charges the company with duping customers," she asks. "Who's at fault?"

The device isn't lying. The device is doing precisely what a noninvasive sensor can do, which is guess intelligently from motion, heat, and a pulse. The lie, if there is one, lives in the marketing — in the leap from "we inferred" to "we know." And that leap is not unique to a ring. It is the entire operating model of an industry that has spent a decade turning shadows into certainties and charging a subscription for the privilege.

If a court agrees that dressing a guess as science is fraud, the ruling won't stop at Oura's door. It will walk straight down the hall to Apple, to Whoop, to Garmin, to every "readiness score" that ever told a healthy person to take the day off. The ring was never the defendant that mattered. It's just the one that got caught in the hallway first.
