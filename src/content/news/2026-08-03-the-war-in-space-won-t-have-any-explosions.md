# The War in Space Won't Have Any Explosions

Here's the good news about a war in space: it probably won't look like the movie. No fireballs, no heroic astronaut with a wrench, no satellite spiralling into the ocean while a general watches on a big screen. Here's the bad news: you won't see it at all, and it might already be the plan.

## What's actually up there

Start with a boring truth. Modern militaries run on satellites. GPS, communications, reconnaissance, targeting, early warning — all of it depends on a fleet of machines quietly orbiting overhead. Take those away and a modern army is a very expensive man standing in a field, wondering which way is north.

That dependence is the problem. When something becomes essential, it becomes a target. In a serious, high-end conflict, the fastest way to blind an enemy isn't to fight their soldiers. It's to switch off their eyes and ears in orbit.

This used to be a niche worry for people with wall maps and too much caffeine. Not anymore. The war in Ukraine, plus rising tension involving China, Russia, the United States and Nato, has dragged space security into ordinary conversation. It's now a mainstream fear — which is progress, I suppose, though not the cheerful kind.

## The danger isn't the bang. It's the silence.

Everyone pictures the wrong weapon. We imagine a missile screaming up from the ground and blowing a satellite into glittering pieces. Dramatic. Cinematic. Also, mostly, not the plan.

The analysts who study this focus on something quieter: "non-kinetic" attacks — jamming, cyberattacks, high-power microwave systems. Nothing gets destroyed. Nothing falls out of the sky. The satellite just stops being useful.

Why prefer the boring option? Because blowing a satellite to bits creates a debris field: thousands of fragments tearing around orbit fast enough to turn a fleck of paint into a bullet. That junk threatens everyone, including whoever made it. Non-kinetic attacks skip the mess. And a mess is evidence. No debris, no smoking crater, no obvious moment of aggression — just a signal that went dead and a shrug about whose fault it was.

It's the difference between smashing a window and quietly cutting the phone line. One gets you arrested. The other gets you a plausible "must be a glitch."

That's the part that should keep you up at night. Not the spectacle. The deniability.

## Five ways to kill a satellite without touching it

Think of a satellite as a person doing a job. To stop them working, you don't have to kill them. You have a menu of rude options, and it's worth knowing what's on it.

The first is to shout over them. That's jamming — you flood the frequency with noise until nobody can hear the satellite's signal over the racket. The satellite is fine. Its message just never arrives, like trying to take a phone call next to a leaf blower.

The second is to blind them. Point a laser at the satellite's sensors and you can dazzle them, or burn them out entirely. A reconnaissance satellite that can't see is a very costly rock. Same machine, same orbit, suddenly useless — the orbital equivalent of shining a torch straight into a photographer's lens.

The third is to hack them. Cyberattacks go after the network itself. Why physically reach a satellite when you can break into the system telling it what to do? At that point you don't have to destroy anything. You just borrow it.

The fourth is to cook them. High-power microwave systems and electronic warfare go after the electronics — overwhelming the network, frying the delicate parts that make the thing tick. No debris, no drama, just hardware that quietly gives up.

The fifth is the creepiest. Co-orbital systems are satellites that can approach another satellite and disable it. One of your machines sidles up to one of mine, close enough to interfere — like a stranger sitting down right next to you on a completely empty train. Nothing has happened yet. But you know something is about to.

Every single one of these can turn off a satellite without lighting up the sky.

## Nobody guards the plumbing

Now the twist most people miss. All this talk of "military satellites" makes it sound like a soldiers-only problem. It isn't. Commercial constellations like Starlink have smeared the line between civilian and military infrastructure until you can barely find it. The same network carrying your video call might be carrying a battlefield order.

Here's why that matters to you personally. A conflict in space wouldn't just scramble military command and control. It would hit navigation, telecoms, weather monitoring, logistics and transport — and, in a detail almost nobody appreciates, banking time-sync systems.

Yes, banking time-sync. Your money doesn't just need a bank; it needs banks that agree on what time it is, down to fractions of a second, so that transactions land in the right order. A lot of that precise timing rides on satellites. Knock that out and you don't get a Hollywood explosion. You get financial systems that suddenly can't agree on whether you paid before or after your rent bounced.

And this is the genuinely alarming bit. We spend fortunes defending the shooting kind of infrastructure — jets, ships, missiles. The civilian orbital plumbing that runs navigation, telecoms and the timing under your bank account? Largely nobody's job. It's load-bearing and unguarded, which is a spectacular combination to leave lying around.

## The war you'll never see

We've been trained by decades of films to fear the wrong thing. We brace for the missile and the fireball, for a war in space that announces itself with a bang and a mushroom cloud of debris.

The real thing is patient and silent. It's a jammed frequency, a dazzled sensor, a hacked network, a satellite that simply sits next to yours and waits. No explosion, no confession, no obvious villain — just the slow, deniable strangling of the systems that tell your phone where you are and your bank what time it is.

A war in space wouldn't end with a satellite falling to Earth. It would end with your everyday life quietly not working, and no one able to say for certain who switched it off. That's not science fiction. It's the plan, and it's a good one — which is precisely the problem.
