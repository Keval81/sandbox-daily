# London Is Running Out of Room at Both Ends of Life

There is a dark joke buried — forgive me — in the latest audit from City Hall. The same city that cannot build enough homes for the people arriving has now confirmed it is running out of ground for the people leaving. London has managed the rare feat of a space crisis at both ends of the human life cycle simultaneously.

The document is called the *Audit of London Burial Provision*, and it is exactly as cheerful as it sounds. Three boroughs — Camden, Hackney and Tower Hamlets — already have no burial space at all. The audit predicts a further 17 local authorities will have none inside their own boundaries by 2040. Hina Bokhari, a Liberal Democrat member of the London Assembly, rounds this to roughly 20 boroughs with nowhere to bury their dead "in 15 years' time." She calls it "a real crisis point." For once, the political language undersells the problem.

## A City With Nowhere to Put Anyone

The mechanics are simple. Many of inner London's cemeteries stopped taking burials long ago. The active sites that remain sit mostly in the outer boroughs — the report points to an east London Muslim cemetery as an example of where Londoners now travel to be laid to rest. So the demand is concentrated in the middle of the city and the supply is stranded at the edges, and the two are drifting further apart every year.

The timing could hardly be worse. Office for National Statistics figures cited in the audit expect the share of Londoners aged 65 and over to reach "just over 40%" by 2040. In fifteen years, two in every five Londoners will be past retirement age. That is the same fifteen years in which twenty boroughs are expected to run dry. As Bokhari puts it, "15 years is gonna go really quickly." She is not wrong, and the demographics are not on anyone's side.

## The Insight: Burial Is the Public Service Nobody Has to Provide

Here is the part that ought to stop you. Under the law, a council **can** provide and maintain cemeteries — but it is not required to. Burial space is discretionary. It is a nice-to-have, legally speaking, sitting on the same shelf as hanging baskets and Christmas lights.

Think about what that means for a broke borough weighing its books. Social care is a statutory duty. Bin collection is a statutory duty. Housing the homeless is a statutory duty. Finding you six feet of earth is a favour. When money is tight — and in London it is always tight — the thing nobody is legally obliged to do is the thing that quietly stops getting done. The burial crisis is not a bug in the system. It is the system working exactly as written.

And notice the strategy on offer to fix it, because you have seen it before. The plan is to squeeze more out of the land we have, and when that fails, to release protected countryside on the edges. That is the housing playbook, word for word. It is the approach that has produced London's celebrated abundance of cheap, roomy homes — which is to say, it hasn't. We are now applying the same logic to the dead and hoping for a different result. Meanwhile the same city announces, cheerfully, "8,000 homes planned for new south London neighbourhood." The living get a new neighbourhood. The dead get an audit.

## The Breakdown: How You Actually Fit More People Into Less Ground

So how do you conjure burial space out of a city that has run out of it? The official guidance from the Greater London Authority offers three moves, and it helps to picture each one.

**Move one: evict the long-term residents.** The guidance suggests re-using graves that are over a century old. In practice this means the very old dead make way for the newly dead — a sort of natural turnover, if you can get past the imagery of it. It is the cemetery equivalent of clearing out the loft: technically those boxes are still yours, but you haven't opened them in a hundred years and the space is worth more than the memory.

**Move two: build bunk beds.** The GLA also recommends increasing grave depth so a single plot can hold multiple burials stacked on top of one another. This is densification, the exact word planners use for cramming more flats onto a plot of land. We are, quite literally, going vertical with the deceased. The studio-flat logic of modern London has finally reached the graveyard.

**Move three: pave the countryside.** When squeezing and stacking aren't enough, the draft London Plan — the mayor's official strategy for how the city grows — says boroughs may need to look to low-quality green belt land, the bits now fashionably rebranded as "grey belt." If you have followed the housing debate at all, you will recognise this move instantly. Grey belt is the phrase you reach for when you want to build on protected land without saying "we are building on protected land." It worked so well for housing that we are now deploying it for cemeteries.

Put the three together and you have the whole plan: reuse the old graves, stack the new ones deeper, and when the numbers still don't add up, quietly release the fields. Densify, then sprawl. It is a strategy with a track record, and the track record is the housing market you already live in.

## The Takeaway: A Plan Is Not the Same as Doing Something

Bokhari wants the Mayor to use his convening powers — the ability to get every borough in one room — to build a London-wide response instead of leaving 33 councils to each panic alone. Her frustration is precise. "There needs to be a plan," she says, "but there needs to be action behind that plan. And I want the Mayor of London to lead on that action... I don't want it just to be something where he just sits back and waits."

The Mayor's office, for its part, says boroughs "should plan pro-actively and collaboratively for the expansion, reuse, and long-term provision of burial space," should "recognise cultural and faith needs," and should use the audit as evidence in their local plans. Every verb in that sentence belongs to somebody else. The mayor has commissioned a document telling the boroughs to make plans, which is not action so much as the paperwork you file instead of it.

This crisis has been visible for a while. The BBC asked "What happens when graveyard spaces run out?" back in October 2024. The story is not new. What is new is the arithmetic finally becoming undeniable: twenty boroughs, fifteen years, forty per cent of the city over 65.

Death is the one appointment in this city nobody misses, and it is now the one service London has decided is optional. A crisis you can see coming for fifteen years and choose not to fund is not a tragedy. It is a decision. London has quietly made it.
