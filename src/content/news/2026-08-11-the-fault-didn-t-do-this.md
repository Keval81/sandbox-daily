# The Fault Didn't Do This

An earthquake is not a moral event. It is a fact of geology, indifferent and blameless. A building that folds in on top of the people inside it — that is a decision, made years earlier, by someone who is not under the rubble.

Keep that distinction in mind, because Colombia's dead this week are trying to tell you something.

## Day Three

At 7.34am on Monday 10 August 2026, a magnitude 7.4 earthquake struck western Colombia. Both the US Geological Survey and Colombia's own geological survey put it at 7.4 — no argument there. The epicentre sat in Chocó, the department on the Pacific coast. People felt it across Colombia and into neighbouring Venezuela.

By early afternoon, President Abelardo de la Espriella went on television and gave the country its numbers: 111 dead, 87 injured, 61 buildings collapsed. He declared a national state of emergency less than six hours after the shaking stopped. As his address went out, rescue crews were still digging through wreckage with people believed alive inside it.

One detail is worth pausing on. De la Espriella was sworn in as president the previous Friday. This was day three. Most new bosses get a grace period — a week to learn where the coffee machine is. His weekend ended with the ground killing more than a hundred people he had just been sworn in to protect.

## Now Look at the Map

Because the map is the whole story.

The epicentre was in Chocó. The nearest major city to it is Quibdó, the departmental capital. Quibdó reported injuries and severe damage to its buildings — but no confirmed deaths. The city closest to the fault, the place that took the rawest energy, is not where people died.

The dead were inland, in cities further from the epicentre. Pereira lost 40 people. The department of Valle del Cauca, which includes Cali, counted 27. Manizales reported at least two. The shaking travelled outward and got weaker — that is how physics works — and yet the body count went *up* as the danger, in theory, went down.

That is not a natural-disaster story. That is a construction story. The fault delivered the same warning to everyone in range. Some buildings listened and some didn't, and the difference between the two is measured in funerals.

## The Mental Model

Think of an earthquake as one enormous shove. The ground grabs everything and shakes it hard for a few seconds. Every building in the area gets roughly the same shove, scaled down the further you sit from the source.

What happens next depends entirely on the building. A structure built and maintained to ride out that shove wobbles, cracks, and stays up. A structure built cheap, built old, or built without anyone checking does the thing you saw on the videos this week: the roof falls in and the walls come down on top of whoever is inside.

Raúl Andrés Buitrago felt it in Pereira. "It started softly, softly, softly, then it felt like it was getting stronger every second," he said. He ran into the city centre — "home to many old buildings," as he put it, which turns out to be the most important phrase in his whole account. "The roofs started to fall in. The buildings collapsed in on themselves." He called them the four longest minutes of his life. The seismic alert on his watch had gone off as he left for work. "There are often little tremors here," he said. "But I'd never felt an earthquake like this."

Old buildings. That's the tell. The quake didn't invent the weakness; it found it.

## When the Numbers Stop Adding Up

Here is where the counting goes wrong in a very revealing direction. The president counted 61 collapsed buildings nationally. Pereira's mayor, Mauricio Salazar, counted 65 collapsed in his city alone. One city reported more destruction than the entire country — which means either Pereira is spectacularly unlucky, or the national tally was already out of date the moment it left the president's mouth.

Cali's mayor, Alejandro Eder, then added at least 20 more collapsed buildings and admitted he was still "trying to map out what has happened." The dead don't reconcile either: the named cities add up to roughly 69, against a national figure of 111. Around 40 deaths belong to places not yet identified.

When the accounting is running this far behind the reality, you are not looking at a freak act of God. You are looking at a system that got overwhelmed because it was never built for the load.

## The Sentence That Says Everything

The most telling moment came from the officials themselves. In Manizales, Mayor Jorge Eduardo Rojas Giraldo told residents: "Do not go back into the buildings yet… stay outside for a little while at least because there could be an aftershock." In Cali, Eder went further, effectively asking citizens to become their own structural engineers: "If you do not feel safe, if you see very large cracks, please leave the building."

Read that again. The official response to a mass-casualty building collapse was to ask ordinary people to inspect their own load-bearing walls and make the call themselves. It is the civic equivalent of your surgeon handing you the scalpel and wishing you luck.

This isn't a swipe at the mayors. In the first hours, with five hospitals in Pereira alone overflowing and crews stretched across at least 15 trapped buildings, there was nobody else left to do the looking. But a country that has to crowd-source the question *will this wall kill me?* has already told you where its real fault line runs, and it isn't underground.

The context sharpens it. Just six weeks earlier, on 24 June, neighbouring Venezuela was hit by a deadly double earthquake and was still reeling from it. This corner of the continent knows exactly what it sits on. The ground here is not a surprise guest.

## The Takeaway

De la Espriella did what new presidents do. He flew to Quibdó, promised rent subsidies for people who lost their homes, and spoke of "a president whose heart aches for his people." Sincere, no doubt, and beside the point. Aching hearts don't reinforce concrete.

The San José church in Pereira came apart on video. So did a four-storey hotel in Cali, and the cathedral in Manizales cracked open too. These were not flimsy shacks. They were the solid, permanent, built-to-last structures of ordinary cities. When those are the buildings falling on people well inland from the epicentre, the honest verdict is not that Colombia was unlucky. It is that the earthquake was only the messenger, and the message was about everything built before it.

The fault will move again. It always does. The only question that matters is whether, next time, the walls have been told to expect it.
