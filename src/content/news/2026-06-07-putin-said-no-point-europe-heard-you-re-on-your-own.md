# Putin Said "No Point." Europe Heard "You're On Your Own."

When Vladimir Putin dismissed the idea of sitting across a table from Volodymyr Zelenskyy on Friday — "no point," he said, with the air of a man declining a dinner invitation — he probably thought he was closing a door. He was. Just not the one he imagined.

Because two days later, on Sunday 7 June 2026, Keir Starmer is hosting Zelenskyy, Emmanuel Macron and Friedrich Merz at Downing Street. Not to beg the Kremlin for a meeting. To talk about what Europe does now that the meeting isn't coming.

## The Setup

Here is where things stand, stripped of the diplomatic varnish.

The war is four years old. Putin has just rejected face-to-face talks with the Ukrainian president. Donald Trump, asked about the prospect, offered that it would be "great" for the two leaders to meet — the verbal equivalent of a shrug — while his attention has, by all accounts, largely been on talks with Iran.

So the British prime minister, the French president and the German chancellor are gathering with Zelenskyy in London. The UK and France are leading something called the "coalition of the willing," an effort to provide security guarantees for Ukraine as part of a peace process. The name is doing a lot of heavy lifting, but the intent is clear enough: if Washington is distracted, Europe will stop waiting to be rescued and start building the lock for its own front door.

## What the Drones Over St Petersburg Mean

The conventional reading of this summit is that it's a consolation prize. Putin won't talk, Trump won't focus, so the Europeans huddle together for warmth and a photo. That reading is lazy, and it misses the only detail that actually matters.

Look at the timing. The same weekend these four men sit down to discuss "security guarantees," a large-scale Ukrainian drone attack hit **St Petersburg** — Russia's second-largest city — including a strike on an oil terminal in the Baltic port. No casualties were reported. The point wasn't casualties. The point was reach.

This is the part everyone keeps politely ignoring. The guarantees being discussed in London are no longer about helping a wounded Ukraine hang on. They are about backing a Ukraine that can put drones over Putin's home city and set fire to his oil. The phrase "security guarantee" used to mean *we'll help you survive*. It now quietly means *we'll help you hit back*. That is a different war, and a different Europe.

And Starmer has more or less said the quiet part out loud. Last month he signed a new security pact with Poland's Donald Tusk and hailed a "generational uplift" in defence ties. He framed Russian aggression as a challenge "not just in Ukraine itself, but beyond Ukraine, impacting on our own countries." Translation: this stopped being someone else's problem a while ago, and Europe has finally noticed.

## The Breakdown

Imagine your neighbourhood has one enormous, heavily armed sheriff. For decades, whenever there was trouble, everyone just waited for the sheriff to show up, sort it out, and go home. You didn't need a lock on your door, really. The sheriff *was* the lock.

Now imagine the sheriff has started turning up late, looking at his phone, muttering that the *real* trouble is happening three streets over — and that he'll "see how things go." When your house is on fire and the guy with the hose says it would be "great" if you and the arsonist talked it out, you stop budgeting for the sheriff. You start buying your own hose.

That's the coalition of the willing. It's not a war cabinet and it's not NATO. It's a group of European houses on the same street deciding to pool their hoses, because the sheriff's enthusiasm has become a variable rather than a constant.

Now, the second piece. There are two completely different conversations happening at once, and people keep confusing them.

The **first conversation** is the brutal, grinding daily reality. Overnight into Saturday, in Ukraine's Dnipropetrovsk region, one person was killed and three were wounded. Russian forces struck three districts nearly thirty times with drones and artillery. Earlier in the week, Russian strikes killed one person and injured fifteen more — three of them children — while Ukraine repelled hundreds of long-range drones. This is the war as a meat grinder. It does not stop because diplomats are flying to London.

The **second conversation** is the one in the room at Downing Street. It's strategic, cold, and about the next decade rather than the next night. How do you make Ukraine permanently expensive to attack? How do you wire European defence together so tightly that an attack on one genuinely becomes a problem for all?

The summit only makes sense if you hold both in your head at once. The men in the room are planning a long-term architecture *while* the thing it's meant to prevent is actively happening a couple of thousand kilometres away. It's a bit like designing the smoke detector in the kitchen that's currently on fire. Uncomfortable, but that is precisely when you find out whether the design is any good.

And then there's Putin's "no point." Strip away the contempt and it's revealing. A man genuinely confident of winning does not refuse to talk. He talks, stalls, and pockets concessions. Refusing the meeting tells you he thinks time is on his side and that he's betting the sheriff stays distracted. The London summit is, at its core, a wager against exactly that bet.

## The Takeaway

For four years, the operating assumption was that this war ends when the United States decides it ends — that the grown-up in the room would eventually broker the deal. That assumption died quietly this week, somewhere between Putin saying "no point" and Trump saying it would be "great" if the two of them sorted it out themselves.

What's replacing it is messier, slower, and far more consequential: a Europe that has stopped asking permission. A coalition of the willing that, going by the smoke over St Petersburg, is willing to back a Ukraine that strikes oil terminals, not just one that hides in basements. A British prime minister openly calling Russian aggression a threat to "our own countries" and signing pacts to match the rhetoric.

Putin wanted to prove that without an American-brokered table, there is nothing. Instead he's about to discover the opposite. The table moved to London, he wasn't invited, and the people sitting at it have stopped waiting for him to show up. The arsonist just learned the neighbours bought their own hoses — and a few of them are pointed at his house.
