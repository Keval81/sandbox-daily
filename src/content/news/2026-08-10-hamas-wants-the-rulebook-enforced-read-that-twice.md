# Hamas Wants the Rulebook Enforced. Read That Twice.

Here is one of the strangest sentences of the decade: the group now pleading with the world to make everyone honour the peace deal is Hamas.

The same Hamas that on 7 October 2023 led an attack on southern Israel that killed roughly 1,200 people and took 251 hostage is, in August 2026, the side saying *yes*. It has agreed, for the first time, to disarm. And when Israel's Prime Minister said no, Hamas didn't storm off. It "reaffirmed its commitment," declared itself ready "to engage seriously and responsibly," and called on the mediators to make sure all parties comply.

Meanwhile, the man who said no is Benjamin Netanyahu — whose closest ally, Donald Trump, called this very plan "a monumental step toward lasting peace and security" barely a week earlier. If you had drawn up the cast list two years ago, you'd have the roles exactly backwards.

## What Actually Happened

The bones of the story are simple. Trump announced a Gaza peace plan in September 2025 — twenty points, published in full on 9 October 2025. An initial ceasefire was agreed that same month, though Israel has kept up strikes on Gaza ever since. In July 2026 the Board of Peace, the body overseeing all this, announced an agreement for the "complete disarmament" of Hamas and other armed groups.

Then came the fine print. This August, a 15-point document appeared, designed to turn the grand plan into actual steps on actual days. On Sunday 8 August, at a cabinet meeting, Netanyahu killed it in one line: **"Israel rejects the 15-point document."** The army, he added, "will not carry out any withdrawal until Hamas is genuinely disarmed."

So the peace plan has a signature from the White House, a yes from Hamas, and a rejection from the one leader Trump considers a friend. That is not a diplomatic breakdown. That is a diplomatic plot twist.

## The Word "Rejects" Is Aimed at Jerusalem, Not Washington

Here is the part most coverage misses. Netanyahu did not reject peace. He rejected a *document*, loudly, in front of a specific audience — and that audience is not Hamas, and it is not Donald Trump. It is the men sitting a few chairs down from him.

Israel goes to the polls within months. The far-right members of his own coalition have called for a fresh cabinet vote to end the Gaza plan outright. National Security Minister Itamar Ben Gvir dismissed the draft as "not acceptable." For a coalition held together with tape and threats, "Israel rejects the 15-point document" is not a foreign-policy statement. It is a firewall.

Listen to how carefully Netanyahu talks about the Americans. He is in discussion with US officials, he says: "They have ideas; some of them are acceptable to us and some are not, and we know how to stand our ground on these matters." That is not the language of a man slamming a door. That is the language of a man leaving it open a polite inch while shouting through the wall for the neighbours to hear.

In plain terms: he is unhappy with the plan but has no intention of upsetting his most important ally. So he performs a rejection theatrical enough to satisfy Ben Gvir, worded softly enough not to insult Trump. It is the political equivalent of breaking up with someone loudly enough for your ex to hear, but quietly enough that your new partner doesn't.

## The "You Go First" Problem

Strip away the theatre and there's a genuine knot underneath.

Hamas will disarm — but only if Israel withdraws first. Specifically, it says the handover of heavy weapons depends on Israel ending "all forms of aggression" and pulling its forces out. Netanyahu wants the opposite order: disarmament first, then maybe withdrawal. And not just any disarmament — he warned against "fictitious disarmament," which one assumes means he'd like the real weapons handed over, not a cardboard box of water pistols.

This is the oldest deadlock in the book. Picture two people in a standoff, each with a gun, each saying, "You put yours down first." Nobody moves, because whoever moves first loses. Every hostage negotiation, every arms treaty, every playground truce runs into the same wall: trust has to come from somewhere, and neither side has any.

The clever bit of the plan is that it was built precisely to dissolve this. Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace's Gaza envoy, describes a process that is "verifiable... reversible, that at each stage can be stopped." Crucially, he says Israel would not be required to withdraw until it can be *verified* that Hamas is genuinely taking steps to disarm.

So the mediator's answer to "you go first" is: nobody goes all at once. You do it an inch at a time, and I watch every inch. Hamas gives up a batch of weapons, an inspector confirms it, Israel pulls back a step, and if anyone cheats, the whole thing freezes on the spot. Any disarmament and withdrawal would almost certainly have to be phased like this — because the alternative, one giant leap of faith by two mortal enemies, has never once worked in human history.

Which makes Netanyahu's objection curious. He's demanding proof of real disarmament before withdrawal. The mechanism he rejected is a machine designed to produce exactly that proof. It's like refusing to buy a smoke detector on the grounds that you're worried about fires.

## The Numbers You're Meant to Forget

While the choreography plays out in cabinet rooms, the ground tells its own story. The United Nations puts the number of displaced people in Gaza at around 1.9 million — ninety percent of the entire population. That is not a statistic; that is nearly everyone, with nowhere to go.

More than 73,000 people have been killed in Israel's campaign, according to the Gaza health ministry — figures the UN considers reliable.

Those are the stakes hiding behind the phrase "the 15-point document." Every week the choreography drags on, the counting continues.

## Two Different Sentences

Let's be clear about what we are watching, because the labels are lying to us. This is not a story about a peacemaker and a spoiler. It is a story about a spoiler who has, for now, decided to look like a peacemaker, and a peacemaker's ally who cannot afford to look like one until after an election.

A senior Board of Peace official said on Sunday that the plan "remains the only way forward." That is the quiet fact under all the noise. Both sides have already agreed, in principle, to the shape of the ending — disarmament, withdrawal, verified step by step. What's left is not the substance. It's the timing, and the timing is hostage to Israeli coalition arithmetic.

Netanyahu isn't saying no to peace. He's saying no to losing his government. Those are very different sentences, and only one of them is written in the deaths of 73,000 people.
