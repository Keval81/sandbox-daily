A losing lawyer has two options. He can change his argument, or he can change his judge. Donald Trump, never a man for half measures, is attempting both at once—and dressing it up as a crackdown on pregnant tourists.

## Two Orders, One Old Grudge

On Thursday, from the Oval Office, Trump signed two executive orders designed to shrink birthright citizenship—the roughly 150-year-old rule that a baby born on American soil is American, full stop.

The first order expands the list of newborns who would be denied automatic citizenship. It targets US-born children of two non-citizen parents where at least one parent belongs to a foreign terrorist group, works for a foreign government, tried to obtain citizenship by fraud, or lives in a US territory where citizenship is not conferred by federal statute. The second order bans "birth tourism"—pregnant women flying to America specifically so the child arrives with a US passport attached.

"This should have happened years ago, but we're taking care of it now," Trump said. He called the courts' earlier ruling against him "a bad decision, very unfair decision," and promised his country was "ending in a different way." The words "in a different way" are the entire story, and he said them out loud.

## The Loss Is the Plan

Here is the part everyone is politely ignoring. Trump already tried this. On one of his first days back in office in 2025, he signed an order to end birthright citizenship as guaranteed by the 14th Amendment. In June, after a long legal fight, the Supreme Court ruled his policy could not stand. Birthright citizenship remained the law. In early July, he moved immediately to ask the Court to reconsider.

So the obvious question: why sign two more orders weeks after losing, using a legal theory the Court just rejected?

Because he is not trying to win both. He is trying to lose one.

The birth-tourism order—controlling who may enter the country—stands a real chance of surviving. The citizenship-denial order, the one that strips a born-here baby of citizenship, is almost certain to be struck down. And the strike-down is the product. A loss in the lower courts is a ticket. It is how you drag the exact same question back up to a Supreme Court you are already, separately, begging to hear it again.

The defeat isn't a risk of the plan. It's the plan.

## The Front Door and the Delivery Room

To see why one order lives and one dies, picture the country as a house with a maternity ward inside.

The **front door** is immigration. Who gets to come in, and why. Here the president genuinely has keys. Miller—Stephen Miller, the homeland security adviser and deputy chief of staff for policy—points to a section of the Immigration and Nationality Act that lets the president set "exceptions and limitations" on who may enter. Gabriel Chin, a law professor at UC Davis, agrees the president "does have some power to restrict people entering the US for the purpose of giving birth." So a bouncer at the door checking whether your holiday is really a holiday? Legally plausible.

The **delivery room** is different. On its wall hangs a rule about 150 years old: if you're born here, you're one of us. And here is Chin again, and this is the whole ballgame: "once a child is born in the United States, I don't think the president has any power to decide that that child is not a citizen."

That's the trick in the two-order shuffle. Order two guards the front door—defensible. Order one marches into the delivery room to un-citizen a newborn—not defensible, and, per Chin, already settled by the administration's recent Supreme Court loss. Trump has stapled a legal order to an illegal one and is presenting the bundle as a single tough-on-immigration package. Some of it, Chin says, might hold up; other parts "raise some serious constitutional questions." Translation: half of this is theatre with a lawyer's stamp on it.

There is one genuinely clever wrinkle. Citizenship for people born in territories like Puerto Rico comes from federal statute, not the 14th Amendment. A statute is easier to attack than the Constitution. So by folding territories into order one, the administration finds the softest wall in the house and leans on it. That part is not stupid at all. It's the tell that the rest is deliberate.

## The Numbers Do Not Cooperate

Every good manufactured crisis needs a scary figure, and Trump supplied one: "hundreds of thousands" of babies born through birth tourism.

Let's check the receipts. The nonpartisan Migration Policy Institute's most generous census-based estimate lands at roughly 22,000 to 26,000 birth-tourism babies a year. Government data cited by the same group counts 9,600 births in 2024 to mothers with foreign addresses. So Trump's "hundreds of thousands" is inflated somewhere between tenfold and thirtyfold, depending on which honest number you use. Even at its worst, this is a rounding error against the millions of babies born in America every year. Chin calls it a "drop in the bucket" and "an unusual phenomenon"—which is a professor's way of saying you have declared war on a puddle.

To make the puddle menacing, Miller reframes it as espionage. People enter "pretending to be a tourist," he says, when their "real reason" is a child who becomes "an automatic citizen"—a child who will one day claim "welfare benefits, the voting booth." The first order's own text warns of "malign foreign actors who attempt to swindle American citizens by taking advantage of the generosity of our Nation," and the administration has previously accused Russian and Chinese adversaries of exploiting birthright citizenship to slip agents into the country.

Set aside the spy-thriller framing for a second. The plan to defeat a foreign sleeper agent is to wait for him to be born, grow up over roughly two decades, and presumably infiltrate the nation via kindergarten. If that is the threat, America has bigger problems than its immigration statute.

## What He's Actually Buying

Strip away the terrorists, the foreign spies, and the imaginary hundreds of thousands, and here is what remains: a policy that will barely dent a genuinely rare event, paired with a policy designed to fail on purpose so it can be appealed all the way back to the bench that just told him no.

The birth-tourism order is the respectable coat. The citizenship-denial order is the thing hiding inside it—a fresh lawsuit, wearing a fresh set of facts, aimed at the same target Trump missed in June. He told us the strategy in five words: "ending in a different way." Not a different goal. A different route to the same one.

So watch which order the courts kill, because that's the one he wanted killed. Trump didn't run the same play twice and hope for a better result. He built a machine whose failure is the fuel, and pointed it at nine people who already gave him their answer once. He is betting they'll blink on the second ask. That isn't lawmaking. It's a man losing on purpose and calling it strategy—and the maddening thing is, it just might work.
