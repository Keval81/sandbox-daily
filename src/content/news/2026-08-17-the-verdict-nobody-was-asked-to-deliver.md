# The Verdict Nobody Was Asked to Deliver

Money doesn't usually hand down judgment. This week it did.

By the weekend, a GoFundMe page for the family of Jason Arday had passed £130,000, gathered from thousands of separate donations in barely two days. It launched on Saturday. Arday, professor of sociology of education at the University of Cambridge, was found dead on Friday. The week before, he had resigned his post amid allegations that he had plagiarised.

The fundraiser was set up by Sir Patrick Vernon, the Windrush campaigner who played a leading role in establishing a national Windrush Day. On Monday at 6pm, the group Stand Up To Racism plans a vigil on the steps of the National Gallery in Trafalgar Square. An open letter defending Arday had already been signed by Vernon, the Green Party leader Zack Polanski, and the crossbench peer Lord Simon Woolley. Meanwhile Cambridge — which had announced an investigation into the circumstances of Arday's "appointment and tenure," meant to feed into a review of how it makes senior hires — is now understood to feel it would be wrong to decide whether to carry on while the family is grieving.

## Two Courts, One Running Much Faster Than the Other

Look at what actually happened here, because it matters more than the sad shape of it.

Two tribunals opened on the same set of facts. One is Cambridge's: slow, procedural, staffed by an institution that can take an entire term to approve a reading list and had somehow managed to open an inquiry into its own hiring before pausing it out of decency. The other is the crowd's: fast, emotional, and equipped with a payment processor. The crowd finished first. It reached £130,000 while the formal process was still deciding whether it was allowed to think.

And this is the thing people are getting wrong. That £130,000 is not a rescue fund. It is a verdict. Thousands of strangers were asked a question — *was this man wronged?* — and they answered it the most British way imaginable, by registering moral outrage through direct debit. Sir Patrick Vernon put the sentiment plainly: "Britain still cares — people, black, white, brown, care and feel angry about the situation." He is right that they care. The trouble is what they were caring *about*.

## The Two Questions We Keep Mashing Into One

There are two completely separate questions tangled together here, and almost everyone is treating them as the same question.

Question one: *Was Jason Arday treated fairly?* This is about press scrutiny, about vulnerability, about whether a man was hounded. Vernon says journalists were told Arday was vulnerable — "someone with autism, a neurodiverse background — that was completely ignored, and he was hounded, sadly, to his death." He argues the case reflects a "higher standard, a higher burden" applied to Black and minority ethnic people "across different sectors, NHS, local government, civil service, private sector," and sees in it evidence of continuing structural racism. Those are claims about how Britain behaves.

Question two: *Did the plagiarism happen?* This is about words on a page. Either passages were copied or they weren't. No amount of grief, solidarity, or anger changes the answer, because it's a question of fact, not feeling.

Now, here is why the £130,000 matters and also why it settles nothing. Money is a magnificent instrument for answering question one. A donation says: *I stand with this family, I think the treatment was cruel, I am angry.* Fair enough. But money is a hopeless instrument for answering question two. You cannot crowdfund whether a footnote was missing. A payment of £20 does not review a manuscript. The wisdom of crowds, wonderful as it is, has never once determined whether a sentence was original — and it isn't about to start.

Think of it like a jury that stormed into the courtroom, delivered a passionate verdict on whether the defendant was a good person, and then went home before anyone read out the actual charge. The charge is still sitting there. Unanswered.

## Why the Charge May Now Sit There Forever

This is where the collision becomes permanent, and permanently uncomfortable.

Cambridge has paused. Not stopped — paused, on the grounds that deciding whether to proceed while a family grieves would be indecent. That instinct is humane. It is also a trap. Because the man at the centre of the allegation is now beyond the reach of any tribunal. He cannot answer, cannot defend himself, cannot be cleared and cannot be found guilty. Whatever the manuscripts say, no formal process can now attach a name to a conclusion without doing it to a dead man and his grieving parents and his young family.

So the university faces a genuinely ugly choice. Carry on, and you are — in Vernon's words — among the people "still taking shots at him, smearing him now" before he has been buried. He calls it outrageous, and on the human level it is hard to argue. Drop it, and the plagiarism question is simply orphaned: never proven, never disproven, left to drift as gossip forever. At least one Cambridge professor has already argued the investigation should continue. You can see the logic on both sides, which is precisely the problem — there is no clean exit.

Vernon, to his credit, refuses to let the story collapse into a squabble over the deceased's bank balance. "There's lots of speculation about how much money Jason has or doesn't have, that's not the point," he said. "He has a young family... he has parents and he has a family that he has supported." On that narrow point he is unanswerable. A widow and children do not become less real because their father's scholarship is in dispute. The £130,000 buys them time and shelter regardless of what any manuscript reveals, and nobody sane should begrudge them a penny of it.

But notice what the money cannot buy. It cannot buy a finding. It cannot buy the university's signature at the bottom of a report. It has closed the emotional case and left the factual one wide open, and no fundraiser in the world can close the gap between the two.

## What the Money Actually Settled

The £130,000 is real, and it is generous, and it has decided exactly one thing: how a large number of people feel. That is not nothing. Vernon says the case raises questions about press regulation, about the University of Cambridge, and about "the state of race relations in Britain today," and those questions deserve the vigil and the open letter and the argument they are getting.

What the money cannot do is the one thing everyone keeps quietly expecting it to do — reach into the disputed pages and tell us what's there. That question is now frozen between a crowd that has already ruled and an institution too decent, or too cornered, to rule at all. It will probably never thaw.

Which leaves us with an awkward monument. The only verdict anyone will ever formally record in this affair is a number on a fundraising page. And a number on a fundraising page, however large and however heartfelt, is a verdict about the people who donated — not about the man they donated for.
